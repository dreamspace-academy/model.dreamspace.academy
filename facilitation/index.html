How teaching is sequenced

Pedagogy is anchored to the stage. The four stages are one continuum with a single moving variable: who supplies the problem.

These are characteristic emphases, not exclusive methods. The stages are one continuum with a single moving variable, who supplies the problem, so direct instruction, projects, tinkering, peer learning and co-creation all appear at more than one stage. Facilitators combine them according to the learner, the subject, the safety requirements and what the work is for.

StageCharacteristic emphasisWhat the facilitator supplies
Explorer Tinkering and guided discovery[5] Materials, an invitation and room to explore. The facilitator keeps the work safe, makes possibilities visible and offers support when it is wanted, without turning exploration into a set task.
Maker Project-based learning[10, 11] A driving question or project purpose, the taught content and technique instruction it requires, and demonstrations. The starting point may come from the programme, the learner, a community or a partner.
Innovator Challenge-based learning[12] A real situation, help narrowing it into something workable, and whatever knowledge the learner needs, without taking ownership of the response away from them or from the community.
Changemaker Challenge-based learning with action, engage, investigate, act Connections, access, and the questions that test whether the work holds up.
A design caution from the literature

A challenge that is defined too narrowly limits the skill development it was meant to produce[14]. The temptation for a facilitator under time pressure is to narrow the challenge until it is really a project, which is a reasonable move at the Maker stage and a mistake at the Innovator stage. Naming the stage before planning the session is what prevents this.

Two delivery modes

Taught instruction

Structured, syllabus-following sessions with stated prerequisites, learning outcomes and assessed work. This is what an institutional partner is buying, and what certification rests on.

Taught instruction is a full mode in its own right. Novices need guidance, and the strongest published critique of discovery-led teaching is precisely that it withholds guidance from people who do not yet have the knowledge to work without it[17].

Open making

Unstructured, self-directed, peer-driven time. It is not assessed by default, though a learner may later choose to submit something made here as programme evidence if it meets the criteria. People work on what they choose, alongside others at different depths.

It is also where a good deal of retention comes from, continuity, peer relationships and room to practise without a deliverable. Systematic review finds peer support outperforming structured teaching in the later phases of maker work[26], and situated accounts of learning describe exactly this mechanism: expertise circulating through a community rather than being delivered[8, 9].

Open making is timetabled rather than left over. Classes are countable and open making is not, so it is protected in the timetable explicitly.

What a facilitator does

Three roles, and they are genuinely different jobs performed by the same person[14].

1

Organiser

Materials ready, tools working, the space safe, the session shaped, the group formed. It determines whether anything happens at all.

2

Teacher

Direct instruction where it is needed, the technique, the concept, the safety procedure. In a first year, more of the time is legitimately content-focused than people expect.

3

Coach

Judging what a learner actually needs: a question, an explanation, a demonstration, more practice, or space to work it out. Running the talk-through. Helping someone read a failure as information rather than as a verdict.

The skill that takes longest to build is knowing which of the three a particular moment calls for. Coaching a learner who simply does not know how to use the tool wastes both their time; instructing a learner who is one question away from working it out takes the learning away.

Mixed groups are the normal case

Because placement is by demonstrated capability rather than age bands, a session regularly contains people at different depths, and, where safeguarding and supervision arrangements allow, at different ages. That needs planning for.

  • Design tasks with a low floor and a high ceiling. The same brief should be completable simply and extendable indefinitely.
  • Use the range. A learner explaining their build to someone newer is doing the talk-through in rehearsal, and it is one of the strongest things available to a facilitator with a large group.
  • Assess against the programme level, not against the room. A learner’s work is judged by the level rubric they enrolled at, never relative to the strongest person present.
  • Do not publish a typical age as a guide to readiness. It reads as a rule. Where an activity carries a legal or safeguarding age requirement, publish that separately and say why it applies.

Supporting facilitators

Facilitator capability is the binding constraint on quality in this kind of education, and it is well evidenced that it can be built deliberately, structured professional development improves teachers’ knowledge, attitudes and beliefs about maker pedagogy, including in low-income rural settings[30].

What that means concretely here:

  • Onboarding covers the stages, the completion standard, how to run a talk-through, safety, and the equipment a facilitator will actually use, the education model matters as much as the machines, and is the part most easily skipped.
  • A shared stock of challenges and projects, so that no facilitator faces a blank page every batch. Authoring new projects is the heaviest recurring task the role carries.
  • Assessment formats, tracking templates and certification guidance are provided rather than reinvented locally.
  • Curriculum fidelity is checked: which learning areas were actually delivered, as a support conversation rather than an audit.

Quality and safety

Certification is only as good as the conditions it is issued under, so two systems sit alongside it, and they matter more, not less, when the same programme runs in more than one place.

Learner assessment

Criteria published before work begins, in language learners can use, applied to comparable evidence. Assessment formats, progress-tracking templates and certification guidance are held centrally and provided to every site rather than reinvented locally.

Site quality assurance

A shared quality framework with periodic review, standard operating procedures, space layout guidance, maintenance, reporting templates, and a curriculum-fidelity check that asks which learning areas were actually delivered.

Safety

  • Machine-use standards for 3D printers, laser cutters, CNC routers, and soldering and electronics work.
  • Ventilation and electrical-setup standards written into the space specification, not added afterwards.
  • Child safety and safeguarding anchored to recognised frameworks, safety first, designed for children, accessibility, inclusivity and equity[53].
  • Quality anchored to the same recognised principles used to judge educational technology at scale: ask why, design for all, empower facilitators, engage the ecosystem, be data-driven[54].

How this holds up across independently operated sites is covered under replication.

What is hard about this

What is still open
  • Soft results are hard to verify. Persistence, judgement, collaboration and confidence are what facilitators most want to certify, and they are the hardest things to evidence. The talk-through and the reflection together are what the academy does about it. They are not a proof.
  • One person cannot facilitate every subject well. Breadth requires rotation, visiting specialists, and a facilitator comfortable not being the most expert person in the room.
  • Open making is invisible in reporting. It produces no completion numbers, so it has to be protected in the timetable rather than left to survive on its measurable value.
  • Judging when a group is ready for an open challenge takes experience. Experienced facilitators make that judgement well. Nobody here has worked out how to describe it on paper yet, which makes it slower to pass on.
