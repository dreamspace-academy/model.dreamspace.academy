The MakerSpace

MakerSpace by DreamSpace Academy

The space the education runs in: a community makerspace built as an innovation and entrepreneurship ecosystem, and what it is deliberately not.

Version 4.1.0 Published 27 August 2026 Updated 3 September 2026
On this page What it isThe four wordsWhy ecosystemThe gap it answersHow the parts connectWhat it is not

What the MakerSpace is

MakerSpace by DreamSpace Academy is a community-rooted makerspace and digital-education hub where people learn, make, solve and earn. It is designed to be replicable, so that the same offer can run in rural and underserved communities under a shared quality framework.

It is one physical space carrying two things at once: an education offer, the structured domains and the journey through them, and an innovation offer, where people work on real local problems using the same rooms, the same equipment and the same people. Those two are not separate departments; they share a floor.

A makerspace is defined by the blend of disciplines it holds together and by the maker mindset it builds, not by the machines it owns[2]. A modest rural unit that gets the culture right is a real makerspace. A well-equipped room where nobody makes anything is not.

The space is not defined by age. A twelve-year-old on their first project and an adult returning to retrain use the same space, the same standard and the same placement process. Where someone starts is decided by what they can already demonstrate.

The positioning and the four words

Stated academically, the MakerSpace is a maker-based innovation and entrepreneurship ecosystem: a community ecosystem that moves people from making, to innovation, to entrepreneurship, grounded in the maker movement and constructionism, and delivered through project-based and challenge-based learning.

Each of the four words is used in a specific sense.

WordWhat it means herePublished grounding
Maker-based Learning by building real, shareable things, the mindset, not the equipment. The maker mindset[2]; the maker movement in education[3]; where knowledge construction actually happens in maker education[4]
Innovation Making aimed at a real local problem, producing something new for that setting. Inclusive making and agency in engineering settings[27]; makerspaces as sites of social innovation[29]
Entrepreneurship Solutions becoming products, services, livelihoods and ventures, supported as a network, not delivered as a final module. Makerspaces and entrepreneurial contribution[6, 32]; the entrepreneurship competence framework[36]
Ecosystem A self-reinforcing network of peers, mentors, partners and funders, not a linear programme with a start and an end. Entrepreneurial ecosystems[34, 35]; communities of practice[8]

Why it is called an ecosystem

A programme has a cohort, a syllabus, a completion date and an alumni list. An ecosystem has returning people, overlapping roles and results that arrive years later through routes nobody planned. The literature on entrepreneurial ecosystems is consistent that what produces entrepreneurial activity is the surrounding conditions (networks, finance, mentors, culture) rather than any single intervention delivered into a vacuum[34, 35].

That is why the MakerSpace invests in the things a course would treat as overhead: open making time that nobody assesses, alumni who come back as facilitators, a room where the person on their first soldering iron can see somebody testing a product. Peer support has been found to outperform structured teaching in the later, build-and-scale phases of maker programmes[26], which is an argument for protecting unstructured time rather than filling it.

The gap it answers

Sri Lanka’s published national statistics describe a specific problem, and the obvious framing is not the one the evidence supports.

19.1%national computer literacy, 2024[58]
10.7%computer literacy in the Eastern Province, against 31.4% in the Western Province[58]
92.6%of internet access in the estate sector is by smartphone, against 3.6% by desktop or laptop[58]

The third figure is the argument for a shared physical facility. Internet access is no longer the binding constraint it once was, but computer-aided design, a laser cutter, a sensor board, a multi-track recording session and a full-stack application cannot be worked on with a phone. Where almost all connectivity is mobile, what is missing is a bench, a machine and someone who knows how to use them.

On employment, the picture is more specific than a general shortage of work. Youth unemployment nationally stood at 23.0% in 2023, roughly five times the all-ages rate, and 58.0% of employment was informal[57]. But the same survey puts the Northern Province at the lowest provincial youth unemployment in the country, 14.9%, against 34.4% in Uva and 33.3% in the South.

So the problem in the east and north is not a simple shortage of jobs. It is a mismatch between what education certifies and what work requires, on top of a wide gap in access to the tools modern work is done with.

Two further pieces of national context sit behind the model without being quoted as headlines. Multidimensional vulnerability profiling records the northern district of Mullaitivu as the country’s most vulnerable, at 72.4% in 2022–23[59]. The north and east carry deprivations that an employment rate alone does not show. And the whole picture sits on a macroeconomic recovery from a severe national crisis, which is the backdrop against which any claim about livelihoods in this period has to be read[60].

Two different instruments

Multidimensional vulnerability and multidimensional poverty are different instruments, measured in different years against different constructs. They must not be shown in sequence as though they were a trend, a reader given both will infer a change over time that neither instrument measured.

What this argument does and does not claim

This site does not claim the highest youth unemployment in the country, because the national survey does not support that for this region. The argument rests on what the data does show: a large gap in access to the tools modern work requires, and a mismatch between what education certifies and what employers need. Both are directly addressed by what a makerspace is.

How the parts connect

The model has four moving parts, and they sit in a fixed relationship.

  1. Maker education, the four named stages of the Maker Journey, open to anyone, running across current portfolio of domains and held to one completion standard.
  2. An open outcome field, where the Maker Journey ends, people go on to a venture, to research, to activism and community leadership, to employment or freelancing, to working inside DreamSpace Academy itself, or somewhere nobody has catalogued. These are examples, and no total is published for them.
  3. The venture pathway, for those, and only those, who choose the venture route: the DreamSpace Lifecycle, three stages with a challenge-based learning thread and four support modes.
  4. Impact. The ventures, services and solved problems that result, reported against the results hierarchy and mapped to the Sustainable Development Goals at target level[64].
A design decision

Not everything funnels toward founding a company. There is good experimental evidence that pushing marginal participants into venturing can lower their income and employment outcomes[41]. Someone who finishes the Maker Journey with real capability and takes a job is a success of this model.

What it is not

Not a tuition centre

Nothing here is graded against a national examination. Work is assessed against what was made, explained and improved.

Not an equipment room

Tools without facilitation, a community and a standard do not produce making. The equipment is the cheapest part of the model.

Not age-banded

There are no age groups, no age-gated levels, and no published typical age. Placement is by demonstrated capability.

Not a linear programme

People enter part-way, leave, return, and take routes nobody designed. The model is built to record that rather than to deny it.
