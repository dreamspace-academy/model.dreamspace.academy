Definitional review

Makerspace

A definitional review of the makerspace — why the term resists definition, what actually separates a makerspace from a hackerspace, a FabLab, a TechShop and a community workshop, and how thin the outcome evidence still is.

Updated 3 August 2026

This is a research review, not a programme description

It surveys how this term is defined in the published literature, what the sources agree on, where they diverge and what remains contested, and carries its own numbered bibliography. It does not describe what DreamSpace Academy runs. Programme policy is on the core pages.

The use of the word here is often narrower than general usage; the short definition and the boundary are in the glossary, under Makerspace. The review date below matters, because the literature moves.

A definitional review. Citations follow IEEE style; see References.

Abstract

A makerspace is a shared physical workshop where people gather to design, build and share artefacts. Unlike the adjacent terms it is routinely swapped for, it carries no membership standard, no required inventory and no governing body — both the reason it spread and the reason the literature struggles to compare one space with another. This article sets out the definition and its looseness, the separate lineages of the space types, the features that actually discriminate between them, the institutional settings and funding models makerspaces depend on, and what the empirical literature does and does not establish.

I. Definition

The dictionary definition, adopted verbatim in European policy work, is minimal: “a place where people can come together to create or invent things, either using traditional crafts or technology” [6]. A widely used research definition is scarcely narrower — sites of “creative production in art, science, and engineering where people of all ages blend digital and physical technologies to explore ideas, learn technical skills, and create new products” (Sheridan et al., as quoted in [14]).

The looseness is constitutive, not a gap awaiting closure. Makerspaces “do not comply with a pre-defined structure and indeed do not need to include a pre-defined set of personal fabrication tools (or by that matter, any of them to be considered a makerspace)” [5]; the discriminating property is social rather than material. The term is consequently a label, not a specification — “more of a label than a well-defined, intentional project. There is no set formula or specification to build a makerspace” [2]. It is also unevenly claimed: among 51 youth-serving US sites, only 53% considered themselves to “truly be a makerspace”, the same sites also calling themselves drop-in spaces (23.5%), teen or youth centres (24%), innovation labs (22%), design labs (14%), hackerspaces (10%), Fab Labs (8%) and science labs (6%), among some three dozen further names [7].

II. Origins

Three lineages converged, and they did not begin together.

Hacker culture (Europe, 1990s). Hackerspaces trace to 1960s counterculture and hobbyist clubs such as the Homebrew Computer Club, formed around 1975. The first is generally identified as C-Base, founded in Berlin in August 1995, conceived as a non-repressive space where people interested in programming and tinkering could meet and learn from one another [5]. The form reached the United States late: NYC Resistor, HacDC and Noisebridge were founded in 2007–08, after visitors returned from Germany [1].

Digital fabrication (MIT, 2001–02). A US National Science Foundation requirement to increase grant outreach prompted Neil Gershenfeld — whose course How to Make Almost Anything drew students from divergent disciplines — to package the lab’s equipment into a standardised, transportable lab. The first was deployed at an inner-city Boston community centre serving underserved youth, with later versions in Costa Rica, India and Norway. The name “FAB LAB”, a wordplay on fabrication and fabulous, was coined in a 2002 paper by Mikhak and colleagues [2].

The maker brand (California, 2005–06). O’Reilly published the first Make magazine in January 2005, founded by Dale Dougherty; the first Maker Faire followed in April 2006 [2]. Makerspace enters circulation here — as a commercial-cum-community brand, not a technical category. A longer prehistory is also argued, running through nineteenth-century apprenticeship pedagogy and Papert’s constructionism, on which 3D printing is a recent chapter rather than the beginning [8].

III. What distinguishes the space types

The types are routinely treated as synonyms. They are not. The discriminating question is what a space is obliged to do in order to keep the name.

A. FabLab — the only type with a formal standard

The Fab Charter (20 October 2012) states that “fab labs share an evolving inventory of core capabilities to make (almost) anything, allowing people and projects to be shared,” that they are “available as a community resource, offering open access for individuals as well as scheduled access for programs,” and that commercial activity “can be prototyped and incubated in a fab lab, but … should grow beyond rather than within the lab” [3]. The Fab Foundation sets four qualifying criteria: open to the public free or in-kind at least part of each week; subscribes to the Fab Charter; shares the common tool set and processes so designs transfer between labs; and participates in the global network [4]. Labs must also employ at least one staff member trained at the Fab Academy, and the designation is centrally controlled by the Fab Foundation [2].

The standard has a price: “$25–$65k in capital equipment and about $15–40k in consumables” [4], corroborated at “$100,000 or more” for a fully equipped lab [8]. Cavalcanti adds requirements of roughly 1,000–2,000 square feet and a tool set including laser cutter, vinyl cutter and CNC equipment, and notes FabLabs typically operate as nonprofits [1]. The network reports more than 1,750 labs across 100 countries [4].

B. Hackerspace — community governance, distinct origin

Hackerspaces are “typically set up from within a community for the community, thus being community-funded and community-managed,” and are “completely independent from each other,” each with its own organisation, ideology and focus [5]. No charter, no inventory, no central body. The differentiator is cultural: they were “envisioned as places of resistance, the breeding grounds of a counterculture opposed to overconsumption, stringent intellectual property, programmed obsolescence, and proprietary devices” [2]. Their audience was high-end programmers and engineers; the resulting “male-centric, technical culture … is problematic and exclusionary for novices,” and the culture of autodidactism they run on “might not be the best for young learners and schools” [2].

C. Makerspace — the residual category

A makerspace is defined by the absence of the FabLab’s obligations and the hackerspace’s governance tradition. It “may contain a few basic craft and woodworking tools or … may offer cutting-edge 3D printers and laser cutters” [2]; Cavalcanti’s contrast is that makerspaces aim to enable many crafts at once, with “well-considered shop layouts, significant manufacturing infrastructure,” organised “along the lines of traditional businesses” [1]. The cost of the flexibility is interoperability: “compared with FabLabs, despite their flexibility, makerspaces have a much harder time comanufacturing products, connecting with each other, and sharing best practices” [2]. The vagueness also “led to confusion for school leaders and teachers,” some counting a room with a table and glue guns as a makerspace, others meaning professional design equipment [2].

D. TechShop — the commercial chain, and its failure

TechShop was the best-known commercial variant: founded in Menlo Park in 2006, paid access to machines with staff support, ten US locations. Writing before its collapse, Blikstein called it “perhaps the best example of an economically sustainable digital fabrication space,” used mostly by inventors and entrepreneurs and with little impact on formal education [2]. That assessment was overtaken. On 15 November 2017 TechShop closed all US locations and filed under Chapter 7 — liquidation, not reorganisation [10]. The reason its chief executive gave is the substantive point: the maker field “is very much a not-for-profit affair, often bankrolled by government or philanthropic organizations,” and a for-profit “was normally not eligible to compete for such grant money or continuing subsidies” [10]. The commercial model was not merely outperformed; it was excluded from the funding base the rest of the sector runs on.

E. Community workshop and the non-Western types

Field surveys fall back on a bare working definition: “an open access space (free or paid), with facilities for different practices, where anyone can come and make something” [9]. Applied to almost 100 Chinese spaces, it yielded five types: the community makerspace; hardware accelerators and incubators — most government-backed spaces, the authors judge, “are more accurately hardware incubators adopting the ‘makerspaces’ label”; kids’ education clubs; open-access university labs; and in-house corporate makerspaces at firms including Foxconn and a state-owned electronics research institute [9]. The Western four-way typology does not exhaust the category.

IV. Institutional settings, and how spaces are funded

Where they sit. Of 51 youth-serving US makerspaces, 35% were in schools, 26% in after-school programmes, 28% in community-based organisations and 22% elsewhere — science museums, libraries, local-government institutions; 77% were nonprofit, 6% for-profit; reach ranged from 50 to 850,000 annual visitors around a median of 450 [7]. In Europe they are proliferating across museums, libraries, schools and universities in three distinguishable practices: importing a makerspace unchanged; tying making tightly to defined learning outcomes and assessment; or mixtures of the two. Because a dedicated room raises problems of access, staffing, scale and hardware maintenance, pop-up and mobile makerspaces — carts, portable kits, trucks — are a recognised alternative [6]. A systematic review of school studies found the same spread (11 of 22 in dedicated school makerspaces, 5 in classrooms, 2 in libraries, 2 pop-up) and drew the corollary: “there is no universally-agreed-upon model for what a school-based makerspace should look like” [11].

How they pay. Across 826 European spaces, subscription dominates. Of the 463 with funding data, 335 (72%) charged a membership fee, 73 (16%) charged by usage time or material, and 55 (12%) charged nothing; data were missing for 363 [5]. Average monthly membership was €20.93, from €4.20 in Malta to €43.20 in the Netherlands [5]. Where fees dominate, the provider is more likely an association or NGO than a public body; other sources include private companies, municipalities, regional funds and institutional budgets and grants, and for library makerspaces three viable sources are set out — grant funding, which preserves independence; embedding in a host institution to share staff and training; and charging for educational activities [6]. The Chinese survey shows a different mix and a franker account of the strain: one in five spaces funded by a parent company, 34% with some government support, one in four with no income at all, relying on volunteers, and 52% naming a sustainable business model as a problem [9]. The European data also record a ceiling: new openings boomed from 2007–08 and rose to 2013, then declined each year from 2014 to 2016, which the authors read as a saturation curve [5].

V. What the evidence reports

No experimental base. A scoping review of two decades of research on digital making with children and adolescents screened 180 publications — 132 qualitative, 25 quantitative, 23 mixed. Among the quantitative studies “most publications featured one-group posttest only designs,” and the authors state that “we could not identify even a single study in the corpus with a fully-fledged experimental design” [12]. A review of school makerspaces concurs: experimental research demonstrating efficacy against stated learning goals “has not yet been conducted,” with exploratory studies providing “evidence of promise” only [8].

Findings do not aggregate. The systematic review of preK-12 school-based makerspaces identified just 22 empirical studies, 17 wholly qualitative, mean 44 participants (SD 50, range 2–223), 21 of 22 published within four years, with outcomes ranging across content knowledge, affect, engagement, twenty-first-century skills, agency and constructs particular to single studies. The conclusion is explicit: “we cannot, at present, contribute to broader theory about how students learn in these spaces” [11]. The same review found some interventions “administered by individuals who have little formal expertise in makerspace instruction,” called this “troubling,” and noted there are few formal training routes and few experienced dedicated makerspace teachers [11].

Product is not proof of learning. Drawing on Piaget’s distinction between success and understanding, “if the learner succeeds at performing a task, this does not necessarily mean that she understands what was done”; therefore “the learner’s having produced something is not enough to ensure that she has constructed knowledge” [15]. The proposed remedy is mediation, not more equipment. Consistently, across differing pedagogical positions “there is general agreement that provision of some scaffolding is more productive than random tinkering” [8].

Who is served. Across the first 39 issues of Make (2005–2014), 85% of covers featured white boys and men (Buechley, as reported in [13]); youth-serving sites look different, with 42% of participants across the 51 surveyed sites White against 63.7% in the contemporaneous US census [7]. The critical literature argues that broadening representation is insufficient without interrogating what counts as making, since “inventing, making, tinkering, designing, are indigenous practices … that originate and occur naturally in particular ecologies” [13].

VI. Limitations

The category is not a variable. Because makerspace names anything from a glue-gun table to a professional fabrication facility [2], and only about half of self-identifying sites accept the label [7], studies of “makerspaces” are not studies of the same object. This is a measurement problem, and it is the direct cause of the failure of findings to aggregate [11].

The evidence base is descriptive, and narrow. With no fully experimental study identified across 180 publications [12] and no demonstration of efficacy against stated learning goals [8], claims of effect on learning are claims of promise; reported benefits to skills acquisition, employability and social innovation rest on “a limited amount of initial research” [6]. It is also geographically concentrated: 60% of the digital-making literature comes from first authors based in the United States [12], and 92% of the 826 catalogued European spaces sit in the EU15, with France, Germany and Italy alone accounting for 53% [5]. Transfer should not be assumed.

The financial model is unresolved. Membership fees dominate in Europe [5] while a quarter of surveyed Chinese spaces run on volunteers with no income [9]; the best-documented purely commercial chain ended in liquidation, on the stated ground that a for-profit could not reach the grant and subsidy base the field depends on [10]. The one type with a published standard achieves comparability by mandating capital expenditure of the order of $100,000 [4], [8] — itself a constraint on who can adopt it.
