How maker learning is assessed

Why artifacts, talk-throughs and reflection give a stronger picture than attendance, and what each one uniquely catches.

Version 4.1.0 Published 27 August 2026 Updated 3 September 2026
On this page Why not attendanceThree sourcesThe talk-throughCompleteness and qualityWhat cannot yet be verified

Why attendance is not evidence

Attendance is the most commonly reported number in education programmes because it is the easiest to collect. It records that a person was present in a room where learning was available. It records nothing about whether any occurred.

The alternative usually proposed is a written test, which for maker learning measures the wrong construct: a learner can describe a soldering technique they cannot perform, and perform one they cannot describe. Assessment in maker settings is nonetheless feasible and well precedented, around three-quarters of surveyed makerspaces assess in some form[21]. The question is what to look at.

Attendance creates an opportunity to learn. Demonstrated, explained work shows what was learned. Only the second belongs on a certificate.

Three sources, each catching something the others miss

The reason for three rather than one is that each fails in a different direction, and the failures do not overlap.

Source aloneWhat it can miss
The artifact only A working object built by following instructions, or with substantial help, with nothing understood.
The talk-through only A fluent explanation of something that was never actually made to work.
The reflection only Well-written self-awareness with no demonstrated capability behind it.

The reflection is the one most often treated as optional, and it is the one with the clearest theoretical warrant: experiential accounts place reflection as the step that converts a specific experience into something usable in the next situation[7]. That transfer is exactly what a certificate is asserting.

The talk-through does most of the work

A short, structured conversation in which the learner explains how and why their work works, and what they would change. Four properties make it the most valuable instrument available to a facilitator.

  • It costs only time. No equipment, no marking scheme, no software.
  • It is subject-neutral. It works identically for a robot, a song and a painting, which compensates for the fact that digital work is far easier to assess automatically than craft work is.
  • It is harder to rehearse than a written answer. Follow-up questions about choices the learner made tend to separate understanding from recalled explanation, not reliably, but far better than a script does.
  • It teaches while it assesses. Explaining a build is itself one of the more reliable ways to consolidate it.

This has become more important, not less, as generative tools have become widely available. A polished artefact and a plausible write-up are now within reach of a learner who understands neither. A conversation is not.

Completeness and quality are separate judgements

The six evidence states establish that work is complete: an aim exists, options were considered, the thing was made, it was evaluated, shared and refined. That is a yes-or-no judgement, and a coarse one.

How good the work is, is a separate question, and one where false precision is easy. Two published frameworks shape how the level rubrics describe rising quality. Structural accounts of learning outcomes separate work that handles one element, several unconnected elements, several related elements, and a generalisation beyond the task[20]. Skill-acquisition accounts describe the move from following rules, through situational judgement, to fluent practice[18].

Revised taxonomies of educational objectives put creating at the top of the hierarchy[19]. That is the order a maker programme depends on, and the one school assessment most often inverts.

A caution about creativity

Creativity is not one thing at one scale. A useful distinction separates the personally meaningful insight, the everyday creative act, professional-level contribution, and field-changing work[23]. Almost everything a learner produces sits in the first two. A rubric that grades against professional novelty is measuring something the programme is not trying to produce.

What cannot yet be verified

Hard results are straightforward: the artefact exists, it works, it was tested. The results that facilitators most want to certify (persistence, judgement, collaboration, confidence) are the hardest to evidence, and maker educators report this consistently.

The talk-through and the reflection together are what the academy does about it. They are not a proof, and the flag stays open on the certification system rather than being closed with a rubric that implies a precision nobody has.
