Why It Starts With Children

The case for sustained engagement through the school years, without turning age into an entry rule for the STEAM programmes.

Why the work reaches into Grade 6 rather than starting at the graduate

One of three essays on how the education and the organisation fit together.

The argument. An organisation building an innovation and entrepreneurship ecosystem reaches down into Grade 6 rather than starting at the graduate. The case below comes from DreamSpace Academy’s own record, including the parts that argue against it.

Evidence marks. Events come from DreamSpace Academy’s own delivery record; decisions from its written model. Grades: 🟢 primary or corroborated across sources; 🟡 partial or single-source; 🔴 asserted, and under validation.

Read §7 alongside §2. The developmental evidence for starting early is real, and it is graded 🟡. It has published counterweights, §7 records them, and the claim is “the window argues for early”, never “early is proven.”

1. Why DreamSpace Academy moved into schools

For its first seven years DreamSpace Academy aimed at people aged 18 and over. Bootcamps, incubation, labs, the venture pathway, all of it pitched at young adults who had finished school.

Then DreamSpace Academy restructured. Its concept report gives the reason in one sentence:

“Intervening at this age proved late for long-lasting transformation.” 🟢

DreamSpace Academy then split its work into Empowerment Inside School and Empowerment Outside School. It designed EIS, the in-school half, as something it had never attempted before: a sustained six-year engagement, Grade 6 through Grade 11, at no charge to underserved government schools, on the school’s own timetable, using challenge-based learning rather than rote instruction 🟢.

DreamSpace Academy concluded that its previous target audience was the wrong end of the problem. The conclusion came from its own results, which showed it was arriving too late. It did not come from a theory.

2. What the evidence says about the window

DreamSpace Academy found the literature afterwards, and it agreed with the finding. So the evidence was not selected to justify a decision already taken.

Entrepreneurial capability is developmentally banded, and the band opens earlier than most programmes start.

  • Abstract business concepts are cognitively gated: children cannot reliably separate capital ownership from labour until roughly ages 12 to 13 🟢; profit understanding is middle-childhood but is instruction-gated on a price-comparison prerequisite 🟢.
  • Saving and selling are accessible from around age six in a concrete, playful setting 🟢.
  • The most on-point randomised trial (BizWorld, ~2,751 children aged 11 to 12) shows robust gains on non-cognitive entrepreneurial skills: persistence, creativity, forward-looking behaviour 🟢.
  • The field has one clean early-versus-late comparison: a randomised field experiment in the final grade of primary school found the same mindset gains, and recorded that the same programme delivered to adolescents produced zero or negative effects (Huber, Sloof & Van Praag 2014). Taken from a literature scan; the full study has not yet been checked.

The mechanism claimed is narrow. The claim is not that children learn faster. An ecosystem needs a disposition to try, to persist, to treat a problem as addressable, and that disposition is movable at eleven and much harder to move at twenty-two. DreamSpace Academy’s four-stage Maker Journey is built on that: Explorer, Maker and Innovator are stages of purpose before they are stages of skill.

3. School: the one place that reaches everyone

The developmental argument says early. A second, entirely separate line of evidence says in school, and it is the stronger of the two because it is about access rather than psychology.

Where the barrier is cost and distance, a walk-in makerspace does not reach the child. In Nuwara Eliya, 26.7% of children who left school did so because the household could not afford transport or learning materials, and 13.3% had no means of getting to school at all 🟢. In Mullaitivu the barrier is a different one, 33.3% had not passed O/L, and 11.1% had to work for the family 🟢.

Neither of those children ever arrives at a makerspace. Both are in a classroom until the day they are not. The school is the only delivery point that reaches everyone, including the people least able to opt in, which is why the in-school half of the restructure is the half that carries the inclusion claim.

The state has also created an opening. Sri Lanka’s curriculum reform makes ICT and “Entrepreneurship & Financial Literacy” compulsory essential subjects in Grades 6 to 9, phasing in from 2026 and reaching the full band by 2029 🟢, while only 19% of type-2/3 schools have IT labs against 72% of top-tier schools, and the technology-stream student/teacher ratio is 35.3 against a 12.8 average 🟢. In the estate sector 1.5% of households own a computer and 92.6% of internet access is by smartphone alone 🟢.

A mandate the state cannot yet deliver is a demand signal. It creates a partner-and-feeder position for an organisation that already has trainers, curriculum and lab equipment in those districts.

The limit: whether the position is contractible (an MoU, a provincial agreement, a recognised delivery-partner status) is an open gap. What exists today is narrower: written Zonal Education Office approvals in three zones (Batticaloa, Kantale, Nuwara Eliya), and a school-screening rule that selects for absence: no ICT teacher, no computer lab, low O/L ICT results, rural or marginalised 🟢.

4. Readiness: advanced modules need a measured baseline

DreamSpace Academy learned the third reason in its own delivery.

Across many sessions, an experienced DreamSpace Academy trainer recorded that the binding constraint on delivery is not equipment 🟢:

“The biggest issue is not the lack of facilities, laptops, internet, or tools… The biggest issue is the mismatch between the expected level of the session and the actual readiness of the participants.”

“In many sessions, less than 10% of the students were actually fit for the planned content.”

A separate diagnostic on interview-selected students found that of roughly fourteen, five could reproduce a demonstrated Scratch password-checker and none built one independently, and the four fastest completers could not explain why the skills mattered 🟢. Completion is not transfer. Attendance decays at the complexity onset.

The regional data points the same way: estate-sector literacy is 81.3% and falling, and the cohort is 92.6% smartphone-only.

A learner who has never used a desktop operating system is not a learner who “just needs the advanced module.” Adding more services on top of an unmeasured baseline reproduces the same decay. The answer is a measured intake baseline, fundamentals-first sequencing, and dose.

Every one of those three is easier to deliver to a child than to an adult, because a six-year in-school engagement has the baseline, the sequence and the dose built into it. A four-week adult bootcamp has none of them, and the evidence on dose is explicit: one workshop produced no significant gains where a multi-session programme did 🟢.

DreamSpace Academy’s analysis names the cheapest high-value fix: measure readiness behaviourally, at intake, instead of asking people whether they know the topic. It costs a pre-task.

5. Supply: children as the ecosystem’s future staff

The first three reasons are about learning. The fourth is about supply, the subject of the first essay in this series.

DreamSpace Academy’s hardest constraint was never labs, funding or curriculum. It was identifying changemakers, because the model waited for already-motivated individuals to surface. The fix was to build motivation through the Changemaker Journey rather than screen for people who arrive with it, which converts discovery from a search problem into a delivery problem. A school programme running Grade 6 to Grade 11 across many schools is a delivery answer to that problem.

The earlier cohorts already show in the organisation itself:

  • Alumni now lead much of the software, media and music work, and two are directors 🟢.
  • When the model went to Erode, Tamil Nadu, it was “delivered by two people who are themselves products of the pathway” 🟢.
  • The reach runs upward as well as downward: DreamSpace Academy’s school work has extended from students to teachers, then to principals and zonal education staff, and most recently toward preschool teachers through a partner that funds preschools in Sri Lanka 🟢.

Two limits on that. First, those alumni staff are Batticaloa’s bench, and Erode is that bench travelling. It is not evidence that a new site can grow its own. Second, the one test of the second claim failed: DreamSpace Mullaitivu closed, and the recorded cause was a custodian-succession failure, the person running it could not continue, no local successor with the interest was found, and the search ran in Mullaitivu only. Funding, operations and capability were not the cause.

The closure also argues for starting early. A model whose continuity depends on one custodian has to grow its next custodians continuously, and that growth has to start early.

6. Limits on “starting with children”

It does not mean the education, or any MakerSpace, is for children. Age does not determine placement anywhere DreamSpace Academy’s programmes run: it is not an entry rule, not a band, and not used descriptively in marketing either, because a typical age printed on a brochure is read as a rule by exactly the people the programme is trying not to turn away. Age is used only where safeguarding and duty of care require it. The STEAM programmes are for everyone. Placement is by evidence, through a placement check.

It does not mean entrepreneurship is taught to eight-year-olds. Entrepreneurship content is gated by prerequisite, not by age. Early stages teach the entrepreneurial mindset through making plus simple sell-and-save loops; profit-versus-revenue, pricing and venture-building attach to later stages, whenever a person reaches them.

It does not mean everyone is aimed at founding a company. Changemaker opens onto an open field of outcomes, not a funnel. The documented routes (an impact venture, research, activism or community leadership, employment or freelancing, and Grow Your Own) are examples, not a boundary, and all are equally legitimate. The evidence base warns specifically that pushing marginal students into venturing can lower their outcomes 🟡, and the model follows that warning.

It does not mean the school programme is a device donation. A laptop in a school with no ICT teacher does not get used. What is delivered is the machine plus a trained facilitator, a curriculum aligned to the national syllabus, zonal approval, and baseline-to-final measurement.

It is not charity delivered into a school. The clearest failure in the record came from that misreading: EIS was terminated at Dickoya Tamil Maha Vidyalayam in 2026, and the progress report says why in the school’s own terms, “They see us as an NGO and want us to buy things for the school” 🟢. When the host institution’s expectation is equipment rather than capability, delivery collapses regardless of student demand. In a region used to hand-outs, a programme offered at no charge is read as a supply of things. Any programme of this kind has to design for that.

7. Evidence against starting early

The early-intervention case is graded 🟡, and it stays 🟡. Two published findings pull against it, and the model keeps both:

  • Non-cognitive-skill effects on later outcomes are heterogeneous. A screen of 9,553 publications (Smithers et al. 2018, Nature Human Behaviour) found prediction intervals spanning negative, null and positive, with small-study and publication bias 🟡.
  • Early gains can fade. An intensive Rwandan secondary reform raised entrepreneurship at one year but the gain faded by three, with employment 6 percentage points lower, and the losses were concentrated among marginal students pushed into venturing 🟢.

The claim therefore reads:

The developmental window argues for starting early, and the one clean early-versus-late comparison in entrepreneurship education favours early. That is not the same as “early intervention is proven.”

Three further gaps are DreamSpace Academy’s own:

  • No Sri Lanka-specific early-age entrepreneurship impact evidence exists, only policy intent. The best low- and middle-income-country evidence is Rwandan, and its transfer to post-conflict, multilingual, rural Sri Lanka is untested 🟢.
  • Sri Lanka does not appear in the maker-education literature at all, across five independent corpus reviews 🟢.
  • A deliberately sceptical search found no clean rural-makerspace-to-income result, which is why the Earn claim is a hypothesis under validation rather than a finding 🔴.

These gaps are also what DreamSpace Academy can offer a research funder. An unmeasured region is an unfunded region. The organisation has an eight-year longitudinal field site, a case-study record, two peer-reviewed papers and a hosted PhD study. Almost no peer can generate this evidence, and DreamSpace Academy can.

8c. Where it runs

Batticaloa: 5 pilot schools in 2025, 2 more added in 2026 at the schools’ own request (7 on the register). Hatton and Nuwara Eliya: Dickoya and Ireby in 2025; six schools in 2026, four robotics and two ICT. Kantale: 5 schools, 2026. Mullaitivu: planned, not implemented, still awaiting zonal approval 🟢.

8d. Target grades: the band drifted upward in 2026

The same review found that in 2026 the target band drifted upward, and DreamSpace Academy’s own trainer recorded it in writing. Of the six Nuwara Eliya schools, Sri Pada College ran Grades 10, 11 and 12 and Fruithill runs to Grade 11, against a stated Grade 6 to 9 target. The trainer’s note:

“The school was giving us a set of students who are not from our targeted grades… the students are adults… the delivery language is sinhala.”

The school’s own explanation is capacity, it “can not give only selected students as their grade 6 to 9 classes have a huge amount of students.” And DreamSpace Academy wrote its own corrective: “Schools should be clearly informed about the target student profile (Grades 6–9) before the programme starts.” 🟢

What the drift shows. The organisation concluded that starting at eighteen was late, designed a Grade 6 to 11 programme in response, and then, at two schools in one hub in one term, delivered to adults in a language its trainer does not teach in. The design intent and the delivered reality came apart at the point of school scheduling, where a partner-delivered programme is weakest. This does not show the thesis is wrong. It shows the thesis is not self-enforcing: the target grades have to be written into the school agreement rather than assumed.

8e. Cautions on the figures above

  • The concept report’s “616 students across 9 schools in 3 regions” is a planning target that includes unimplemented Mullaitivu, a plan, not delivered reach.
  • The Story Deck’s headline school figures (50+ schools, 5+ zonal offices, 2,000+ students, 300+ teachers) are self-reported and recorded as “DreamSpace Academy states”, never as verified counts.
  • Cohort totals have never been de-duplicated, so no aggregate across programmes is defensible.
  • Some programme records were filed under the wrong hub, month or grade, so every figure above was checked against the content of its document rather than where it was filed.
  • Mid-term and final assessments are marked Mandatory in DreamSpace Academy’s own document checklist and do not exist in the 2026 folder. The baseline-to-final measurement described in §3 is the design; for 2026 the second half of it is currently missing.

Funding. EIS runs at no charge to schools and is co-funded by at least three partners, each contributing part of the cost: IFNB (Germany), Micro-Tech CNC Pvt Ltd (India) under a signed agreement, and the Sabrina Yousuf Foundation 🟢. The programme also states a minimum monthly running requirement per hub, covering staff, infrastructure and materials; it is recorded as stated and not analysed here.

9. Summary of the argument

DreamSpace Academy decided to work with children after concluding, from its own results, that starting at eighteen was late. The developmental literature it found afterwards agreed, with caveats the academy has kept. Three further reasons support the decision: the school is the only place that reaches the child whose barrier is transport money; readiness, not equipment, is the binding constraint on every programme DreamSpace Academy runs, and only a multi-year in-school engagement has the baseline, sequence and dose to fix it; and an ecosystem that cannot grow its own custodians closes when one person leaves, which DreamSpace Academy has already seen happen once. On this model’s own logic, the children in a Grade 6 ICT class in Kantale are the ecosystem’s future staff. The organisation cannot yet prove the rate at which that conversion happens.

A note on sources

Delivery evidence comes from DreamSpace Academy’s own record of the in-school programme, the concept report, the thirteen-phase standard operating procedure, locations, reach, funders and staffing, together with the 2026 re-examination that added the enrolment register, the hub-by-hub delivery record and the document checklist. Also the funder history, and the case studies on the terminated estate-sector site and on learner readiness. The model material is the root-cause analysis, the identity specification, the DreamSpace Lifecycle, the Changemaker Journey specification and the curriculum, and the recorded decision that removed age from the model.

Statistics resolve to their publishers: the Department of Census and Statistics Labour Force Survey 2023 and Computer Literacy Bulletin 2024, UNDP and OPHI district profiles 2023, and the World Bank Sri Lanka Development Update (October 2024). Intervention evidence is cited to the published studies named in the text; the full list is on the references page.

Some figures circulating near this material are refuted, and none of them appears here.

Two framings to avoid. The North and East do not have Sri Lanka’s worst youth unemployment, the Northern Province has the lowest, at 14.9%; the real constraints are underemployment and informality in the East, and entrapment in the estate belt. And the multidimensional vulnerability index (2022 to 2023) and the multidimensional poverty index (2019) are different instruments, and must never be presented as a time series.
