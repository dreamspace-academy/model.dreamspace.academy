How DreamSpace Teaches

The Changemaker Journey, Definition of Done, pedagogy axes and delivery lessons that make up DreamSpace Academy's method for the STEAM programmes.

It makes a case from DreamSpace Academy’s own record, states the counter-argument, and marks how well each claim is evidenced. Where it describes what the academy runs, the core pages are the standing description and this is the reasoning behind them.

The method, the standard, and what the delivery record changed

One of three essays on how the education and the organisation fit together.

In brief. DreamSpace Academy’s written method for the STEAM programmes fixes some things and leaves others fluid. It sets how a piece of work is judged finished and how a person progresses, and it has changed in response to the delivery record.

Scope. The STEAM programmes are one part of the academy’s education. The academy also runs business and entrepreneurship trainings, digital security training, and workplace and teacher training. The method below is for the STEAM programmes; the rest of the academy’s teaching is not yet written up as a method in the same detail.

Evidence marks. The specification comes from DreamSpace Academy’s written model; the delivery evidence comes from its own record of what was actually run. Grades: 🟢 primary or corroborated across sources; 🟡 partial or single-source; 🔴 asserted, and under validation.

1. How the work is organised: pillars and verbs

DreamSpace Academy stands on Education, Innovation, Entrepreneurship, on a foundation of research: the intent is that what the academy runs rests on evidence, though that evidence is uneven. Peacebuilding runs as a lens across all three rather than as a fourth pillar.

The STEAM programmes, wherever they run, offer four verbs:

VerbWhat it meansEvidence footing
LearnDigital education, AI literacy, electronics and fabrication, taught through challenge🟢 well-evidenced
MakePrototype, 3D printing, CNC and laser, robotics, electronics workbench🟢 well-evidenced
SolveTurn real local challenges into projects🟡 framing-supported
EarnIncome and work, in three tiers, see below🟡 / 🔴

Earn is the weakest of the four. Tier 1, a skilled maker selling self-made products for small income, is near-term and low-risk; making produces sellable output and no causal claim is needed 🟡. Tier 2, a sustained livelihood, by either the entrepreneurship route or the employment or freelancing route, is a hypothesis to measure, not a result to assert 🔴. Tier 3, the venture employing, contracting or buying locally, is the most caveated tier, not a stronger one: no employment count exists anywhere in the record, so no jobs-created figure is published.

The four verbs are threads that run inside every stage. They are not a sequence, and nobody graduates from Learn into Make.

2. Stage and Level: two separate measures of progress

The Changemaker Journey, the progression used in the STEAM programmes, separates two things that almost every other framework welds together.

  • Stage = purpose. What the making is for. Named, never numbered, never graded. Whether the academy’s other teaching uses the four stages has not been decided.
  • Level = depth of capability within one track. Numbered, and attached to a course, never to a person.

The four stages of the Changemaker Journey:

StageIdentityCore questionEnter when
ExplorerI find out what’s possible“What can I do?”Walk in, always open
MakerI turn ideas into things“Can I make something?”Chooses a track
InnovatorI take on real problems“Can I make it better?”Skilled in ≥1 track
ChangemakerI change my community“Can I make a difference?”Has produced a grassroots innovation

A Changemaker’s documentation is not better than a Maker’s. It is documentation for a different purpose. Stages are therefore never compared on a competency grid; levels within a track are.

Levels belong to courses. Robotics & Intelligent Systems Level 3 is a course a learner enrols in. A person’s depth reading is their Maker Profile, Robotics 4. AI 2. Music 1, which is the unit of longitudinal record. Level counts are derived, never chosen: anchor the entry point (no prior making) and the exit point (Skilled Maker, independently completes a full piece of assessed work on a self-chosen project), then divide by the volume of one deliverable block. The documented robotics arc comes out at 4 Units × 48 hours ≈ 192 notional hours ≈ 4 SLQF credits, giving four levels, because that domain has four genuine capability thresholds.

Movement has a default and an override. Every track publishes its default order, so a beginner always knows where they are. Any learner can skip ahead by passing the placement check of the course they want to enter: evidence beats waiting, and prior learning counts however it was acquired.

Age does not determine placement. It is not an entry rule, not a band, and not used descriptively in marketing either: a typical age printed on a brochure is read as a rule by exactly the people the programme is trying not to turn away. Age is used only where safeguarding and duty of care require it. The STEAM programmes are for everyone; placement is by evidence.

The earlier scale. Until July 2026 the model specified a five-level scale banded by age. That ladder welded five variables (age, cognitive depth, course depth, certificate, cohort) into one word, which produced an inverted difficulty order, an unfixable middle trio and a permanent dispute about the bands. Separating Stage from Level removed all three problems.

3. Definition of Done: the one fixed standard

There is no fixed making loop. Making flows however it flows: some learners aim first, some tinker with materials until an idea appears, and trainers sequence sessions however fits the group. The field works the same way. None of the established maker bodies examined publishes a loop: Maker Ed’s Approaches, Fab Academy, NuVu and the Dutch Platform Maker Education.

What is fixed is the completion standard. An assessed piece of work is done when six evidence states exist, produced in any order:

EvidenceMeans
AimThe intent is stated (a problem, a brief or an idea) with criteria to judge by
OptionsMore than one route was considered before committing
MadeThe thing exists
EvaluatedIt was judged against the aim’s own criteria
SharedThere is a record and an audience saw it
RefinedAt least one improvement was made from what evaluation or sharing surfaced

These are checklist items, not steps. No order is prescribed or assessed. Explorer work is exempt: it is unassessed by definition, run as hands-on exploration with learner-set goals. The standard applies exactly where certification applies: Maker stage and above.

The six categories are the intersection of the engineering (NGSS), arts (NCAS) and maker (Papert / Tinkering Studio) traditions, and each carries a one-line gloss per track so the same standard fits every track:

Robotics & Intelligent SystemsMusicVisual Arts
AimWhat problem, for whomWhat piece, what feeling, what occasionWhat idea, what response wanted
OptionsSketch 2 or 3 mechanismsTry several melodic / rhythmic ideasThumbnails, references, compositions
MadeBuilt and programmedComposed, arranged, recordedThe work produced
EvaluatedTested against spec, does it work?Rehearsed and listened backThe crit, does it read as intended?
SharedDemo + build logPerformance + session notesExhibit + process journal
RefinedRedesigned from test dataArrangement revised, re-recordedReworked from the crit

What the standard replaced. Until July 2026 the model described a fixed six-step loop and claimed it was its best-evidenced part. That claim was wrong. The loop opened with “Identify a problem”, which is engineering-shaped and fails for music and art, and its claim to run at every stage was contradicted by field evidence at Explorer.

4. Certificates: three pieces of evidence

A certificate at any level requires all three:

  1. The artifact, the hard result; Definition of Done met.
  2. The talk-through, a short structured conversation in which the learner explains how and why the work works. This is Piaget’s clinical-critical method as adapted for constructionist assessment 🟢. It costs only trainer time, works identically for a robot, a song or a painting, and compensates for the assessability gap between digital and craft tracks.
  3. The reflection, what did I learn, what would I do differently. The direct evidence for the soft results the competency grid certifies.

This is not yet verified. Soft results are hard to verify, and the Dutch platform’s own teachers say so 🟡. The talk-through and reflection pair is DreamSpace Academy’s mitigation, not a proof, and certification of soft results stays marked as unverified until DreamSpace Academy’s own delivery data says otherwise.

5. Teaching: delivery mode and inquiry pedagogy

Delivery mode. Two, and both are protected:

  • Taught Instruction, structured, syllabus-following, tuned to the maker model. This is what a university or corporate partner buys.
  • Open Making, unstructured, self-directed, peer-driven. The model treats it as the retention engine 🟢, though retention has not yet been measured here, and its instruction is explicit: protect open making time.

Inquiry pedagogy, anchored to Stage:

Explorer     Hands-on exploration                       no problem given yet; the materials invite it
Maker        Guided Project Work                        the facilitator sets the brief
Innovator    PBL (Project-Based), inside a CBL frame     the learner chooses a problem within a domain
Changemaker  CBL + action                               the community supplies the problem; Engage, Investigate, Act

Problem-Based Learning is dropped from the model entirely. PBL means Project-Based.

Challenge-Based Learning is a thread, not a stage. CBL is how DreamSpace Academy teaches at every point on the pathway: lab rotations, interdisciplinary workshops and challenge projects appear before discovery, during innovation and inside venture building.

6. Innovation & Enterprise: the cross-cutting strand

The strand runs through the curriculum and the venture pathway in three progressive areas:

  1. Research & Development, make it work. Research → hypothesis → prototype → test → document → iterate. (The Solve verb.)
  2. Entrepreneurship, make it matter. Opportunity → customer discovery → value proposition → MVP → pitch → network. (The Earn verb.)
  3. Business & Financial Literacy, make it last. Costing, pricing, bookkeeping, marketing, operations, quality.

In one line: R&D → Entrepreneurship → Business, make it work, make it matter, make it last.

Entrepreneurship content is gated by prerequisite, not by age. The evidence base is developmental: abstract business concepts (ownership, profit-versus-revenue, enterprise structure) do not reliably land before roughly ages 10 to 13 🟢; saving and selling are accessible from around six in a concrete, playful setting 🟢; profit understanding is gated on a price-comparison prerequisite 🟢. The best randomised trial in this space (BizWorld, ~2,751 children aged 11 to 12) shows experiential entrepreneurship education moves mindset (persistence, creativity, forward-looking behaviour) but not business knowledge 🟢.

What that literature identifies is a schema prerequisite; Cognitive Load Theory names why content pitched above a learner’s existing schemas collapses. Age is the proxy those studies used, because they studied school cohorts. DreamSpace Academy measures the thing itself, through the placement check. So early stages teach the entrepreneurial mindset through making plus simple sell-and-save loops; profit-versus-revenue, pricing and venture-building attach to later stages, never to a birthday.

7. Tracks taught within the STEAM programmes

The tracks currently documented in the STEAM programmes are below. Some of them, such as software and AI, also overlap with the academy’s digital skills training, and where they belong has not been decided.

#TrackFooting
1Maker & Fabrication, electronics and soldering, 3D printing, CNC/laser basics, robotics foundations🟢
2Digital Education, digital literacy, hands-on skills, work readiness, responsible digital use🟢
3AI Programmes, intro to AI and generative AI, responsible/ethical use, hands-on AI, AI-assisted creativity🟢
4AI + Electronics, sensors and automation, hardware plus smart-system projects🟢
5Software & Tech-Entrepreneurship, coding → web/app → shipping a product, freelancing, tech venture🟢 curriculum; delivery uneven
6Creative Arts, visual art and craft, music, media🟡 real delivery, not yet a structured multi-level curriculum

The AI track has a five-year maturation visible in the archive: No-Code AI (2021) → AI Camp (2023) → AI & Data Science Fundamentals (2024) → GenAI/LLM JumpStart (2025) 🟢.

The curriculum specification is a floor on what DreamSpace Academy teaches, not a description of it. It is built largely from written curriculum documents, and those documents have been shown repeatedly to under-record delivery, the electromechanics spine was being taught on video in May 2019, more than three years before it was first written down, and cardboard prototyping is absent from the written lab spine while the team confirms “we still do cardboard based prototypes for workshops.” A gap in the written curriculum means not yet recorded, never discontinued, and the curriculum is more than the documents list.

DreamSpace Academy’s handbooks formalised a curriculum that was already running. They did not design one. Practice came first and documentation after. That is also why the cheapest step survived: cardboard replicates without capital and was never in the document to be cut.

8. Sessions in practice

The delivery record shows what the specification looks like in a session:

  • A standard session in the robotics batches is 180 minutes. The robotics arc is a four-level innovation journey: Basics of Electronics (3 months, atoms, current, battery/LED/copper-tape circuits, breadboards, series and parallel, buzzers, motors, LDR) → Electronics in the Factory / Microcontroller (sensors, Scratch/mBlock, Arduino, 3D printing introduced) → IoT (ESP8266/ESP32, cloud platforms) → AI (machine learning, vision, sound, face and colour recognition). Each level ends in a themed build, an Innovation Farm, a Mini Factory, a Space Station 🟢.
  • Learners write code in Tamil, as a pedagogy step, with a “Human Arduino” role-play in which they physically act out the program before typing it 🟢.
  • An EIS session has a published shape: icebreaker 10 minutes, theory 30%, hands-on 60%, reflection 10%. ICT runs 20 hours over four months, two sessions a week 🟢.
  • Student projects are problem-driven and local, not exercise-driven: a landmine-detection autonomous robot, lake flood monitoring and alert, rain-activated clothes protection, energy-saving library lights, an early flood-warning LED, a DIY animal repellent for crop protection 🟢. The project list reflects post-war, flood-prone Batticaloa.
  • Assessment is bracketed by measurement: EIS runs a baseline assessment before delivery and a baseline-versus-final comparison at the end, with monthly, mid-term and final impact reports 🟢.

9. What makes someone a Changemaker?

Progress inside the top two stages is marked by named milestones, recorded and never graded.

  • Innovator: problem chosen (real, outside the classroom) → first user feedback → solution validated with the person it was for.
  • Changemaker: grassroots innovation produced, the converting milestone → route chosen → measured impact beyond the programme.

A grassroots innovation has three conditions, and all three must hold:

The problem came from the community, a real user who is not on the project team has tested it, someone depends on the outcome.

The output can be a product, a service or an initiative; the form does not decide it.

The gate is deliberate: not every Innovator becomes a Changemaker. A technically excellent build with no community origin and no real user who is not on the project team leaves its maker an Innovator, a complete destination, not a stalled one.

The model applies the same test to its own record. When DreamSpace Academy re-screened past projects catalogued as innovations against this tightened definition, few passed. A larger group could not be decided because the record stops at the build, and several were innovations but not grassroots innovations.

Changemaker opens onto an open field of outcomes, not a funnel. The documented routes are examples, not a boundary, and all are legitimate: an impact venture, research, activism or community leadership, employment or freelancing, and Grow Your Own. Not everything funnels toward founding a company: the evidence warns that pushing marginal students into venturing can lower their outcomes 🟡.

10. What the delivery record forced the method to change

DreamSpace Academy frames its own history as design-based research (iterate, observe, refine, build theory) and the record shows four iterations of the model rather than four programmes:

  1. Exposure-oriented bootcamps, awareness and first skills; limitation: no continuation pathway after the event.
  2. Cohort-based ideation, ideas generated; limitation: weak execution continuity, the idea-to-build gap.
  3. Emerging maker peer network, mentorship culture begins; insight: peer learning proved more powerful than structured teaching in the later build and scale stages.
  4. Hybrid ecosystem model, structured plus organic; continuity improves for some.

The core finding: maker-to-entrepreneur pathways cannot be delivered as linear programs; they emerge through iterative ecosystem design that combines structured interventions with organic, peer-driven learning networks. The decisive shift is from programme delivery to ecosystem facilitation.

A trainer’s own written reflection across many sessions reshaped session design 🟢:

“The biggest issue is not the lack of facilities, laptops, internet, or tools… The biggest issue is the mismatch between the expected level of the session and the actual readiness of the participants.”

“In many sessions, less than 10% of the students were actually fit for the planned content. Because of this, I often had to move away from the planned curriculum and simplify the session on the spot.”

“In one Python session, I gave a small task and nobody completed it. They had laptops, internet, and support, but the effort and drive were missing.”

“The most important feedback is from the students who stopped attending. We currently do not know why they left.”

The resulting design instructions:

FindingChange
Readiness mismatch is the binding constraint, not resourcesDesign to measured readiness; sequence fundamentals-first
Self-reported familiarity is an unreliable screenReplace the “do you know it? / yes” phone check with a behavioural pre-task and an explicit commitment statement
Attendance decays at the complexity onsetShort tasks, demonstrations, group work and immediately usable tools; ease project difficulty rather than adding content
Sessions of 2 to 3 hours outrun attention and trainer energy90-minute to 2-hour blocks
Hands-on ≠ automatic learningMaking without readiness or drive did not produce learning, so the constructionist claim carries that limit
Feedback is survivorship-biasedBuild a dropout-feedback instrument; completer feedback is vague-positive and not the signal
Adult sessions fail on misalignmentNegotiate the deliverable with participants or their superiors before designing the session

These are findings with a reform list, not reforms already made. The observe-and-analyse half of the design-based-research loop has run; the intervene-and-re-test half has not.

11. What DreamSpace Academy counts

Per course: learners enrolled, certification rate at the talk-through, progression to the next level or stage. Per stage: milestone attainment, and the routes Changemakers take. Per learner per term: Definition-of-Done completions, the unit of assessed output. And the Maker Profile as the longitudinal record.

Two things are not measured. The conversion rate from Candidate to Changemaker has no denominator yet, and drop-out is unmodelled at every stage. A pathway with no exit path cannot model retention, and every published outcome currently comes from the people who stayed.

12. Open questions

  • Earlier certificates: how certificates issued under the earlier scheme map to the current stages has not yet been settled.
  • Per-track level counts, derived when each track is authored; robotics first.
  • Milestone names, a working set, not yet delivery-tested.
  • Assessable artifacts per level, the curriculum matrix is an indicative placement of real delivered content onto the level spine, not yet finalised course specs.
  • Certification and soft results, which remain unverified.

A note on sources

The method specification comes from DreamSpace Academy’s written model: the Changemaker Journey and Definition of Done specifications, the curriculum, the DreamSpace Lifecycle, the innovation and enterprise strand, and the controlled vocabulary. The delivery evidence comes from its own record of what was run: the training-delivery and curriculum inventories, the account of how the model changed over time, and the case studies on learner readiness.

Where a decision is cited it is a recorded decision of the organisation, not an interpretation of one. Short definitions of every term used here in a specific sense are in the glossary.
