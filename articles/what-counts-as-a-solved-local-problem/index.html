What counts as a solved local problem

Three tests, why a technically excellent build can fail all of them, and why a definition this strict makes the goal harder on purpose.

Version 4.1.0 Published 27 August 2026 Updated 3 September 2026
On this page The problem with the wordThree testsWhat happened when they were appliedWhere the term comes fromWhat a strict definition costs

The problem with the word “impact”

Every organisation in this sector can produce a list of projects. Very few can say which of them changed anything, because the words used to describe them do not distinguish between four quite different achievements.

A learner project is valuable. A working prototype is a real accomplishment. A prototype that someone outside the building has actually used is rarer and better. And a grassroots innovation, the fourth rung, is a different kind of thing entirely.

The stated goal is one hundred local challenges solved by local people, by 2050. A goal like that is only meaningful if the countable unit is defined precisely enough that the definition cannot be moved when the count is disappointing.

Three tests, and all three must hold

1

The problem came from the community

Somebody who lives with the problem named it. It fails when the problem was set for the occasion, a competition brief, a funder’s theme, a lab exercise, or the maker’s own curiosity. Who relayed it is not the test: a mentor naming a real community problem passes.

2

A user outside the makerspace has tested it

Not a classmate. Not the person who built it. Someone with no stake in it going well.

3

Someone depends on the outcome

There is a real consequence if it stops working. This is what separates a solution from a demonstration.

The form does not matter, a product, a service or an initiative all qualify. The three tests are about origin, contact and stake, not about technology.

What happened when they were applied to the academy's own record

Past projects that had been catalogued as grassroots innovations were screened against these three tests.

Most failures were on the first test

Among the entries that definitively failed, the largest group failed on problem origin: the problem had been framed by a competition or a lab rather than by the community. Several of these were technically excellent builds.

The largest group could not be decided

A substantial bloc could not be decided at all, not because the work failed, but because the record stops after the build. Whether a user existed was never documented, so it cannot now be established either way.

The undecided group

“Not known” is a different result from “it failed”, and it points at something fixable: the record ended where the interesting question began. It is also a practical argument for the sell loop, a sale leaves a buyer, a price and a date behind it without anyone having to remember to record them.

A note on earlier figures: published counts of “changemakers” from before this gate cannot be read as counts under the definition on this page without re-screening, and are not presented that way.

Where the term comes from

“Grassroots innovation” is not a phrase coined here, and using it carries obligations. The most-cited definition describes innovation driven by social need and ideology rather than profit, rooted in the community that experiences the problem, and resourced through grants and voluntary effort rather than markets[46]. A South Asian strand documents innovation by knowledge-rich but economically poor people, built on unaided innovation and traditional knowledge, and usefully names empathetic innovators, people who solve a problem they did not personally suffer[47].

Two things follow from taking the source seriously.

  • The empathetic case is legitimate. A learner does not have to have lived the problem. They do have to have got it from someone who did, which is exactly what the first test checks.
  • There is a real difference worth naming. The grassroots-innovation tradition positions itself partly against the market economy. This model treats a grassroots innovation as a possible waypoint toward an impact venture. That is a real difference of orientation rather than a detail, and it is stated here rather than left implicit in the borrowed term.

What a strict definition costs

A looser definition would produce a bigger number. What the strict one gives up:

A strict gate means many good projects do not meet it. A technically excellent build with no community origin and no outside user leaves its maker an Innovator rather than a Changemaker, a complete destination in its own right. It means the academy's historical figures need re-screening before they can be reused. And it means the goal of one hundred by 2050 is harder than it would otherwise be.

A strict definition is what allows the number to stand when someone asks how it was reached.

There is also a live tension that has not been resolved. If the real historical rate of solved local problems turns out to be much higher than assumed here, then one hundred by 2050 is too soft and should be revised upward, while a tightened definition pushes the other way. Definition and target therefore have to be decided together. Adjusting one alone sets a target against a definition that no longer applies.
