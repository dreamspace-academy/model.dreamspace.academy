How the work is funded

What pays for the work now, what the model is trying to become, and the rules for describing work that reached the academy through somebody else.

Version 4.5.1 Published 27 August 2026 Updated 9 October 2026

Where the money comes from

Three kinds of source have paid for the work since it started, and all three are still running.

Grants and partnerships

More than twenty since 2019, spread across funders, among them United Nations Volunteers, Search for Common Ground, Peace Direct, IREX, USAID through Global Communities, Lenovo, GIZ, the United States Embassy and ChanceMaker. BOOKBRIDGE, which incubated the academy in 2018, provided a loan rather than a grant.

Earned revenue

Services, trainings and trading, sold at a price, including client projects carried out by the media lab and the software lab. That income has done two jobs: running the organisation, and paying for work that had no funder behind it.

The founders’ own capital

The co-founders have put their own money into the organisation.

The three are not ranked

DreamSpace Academy does not describe itself as grant-led, and does not describe itself as independent of grant funding. Both are claims about which source leads, and the relative shares have never been measured. Until they are, the academy uses neither description.

DreamSpace Academy calls itself a social enterprise as a statement about structure, not about funding. The term does not claim independence from grants, and it does not admit dependence on them.

Early-stage capital in this country is scarce, the ecosystem is concentrated in the capital, and domestic venture capital is immature, so young organisations here run on bootstrapping, angel capital or foreign grants[60]. That is a property of the ecosystem, and it shapes the options available to anyone working in it.

What the funding model is meant to become

The academy aims to become an ecosystem that funds a growing part of its own work, rather than an organisation that raises the same money again every year. Four parts are meant to compound.

Units that earn

A community unit sells services and products locally, to local people, at prices those people can pay, which is how the organisation that incubated the academy expected its own loan to be repaid.

Ventures that trade

Ventures that come out of the pathway create local economic activity, and some of that value returns as mentorship, partnership and demand.

Capital that recycles

Loans rather than only grants, so that a repayment funds the next founder instead of ending the relationship.

Alumni who return

People taught here come back as trainers, mentors and directors. This is the one part of the loop already visibly happening.

Intent and current state

DreamSpace Academy already earns, from the trainings it provides and from client projects carried out by its media lab and software lab. The organisation has not reached self-financing, with earned revenue and a franchise share paying for the work as a whole. One part of the work has reached it: the media lab paid its own way, and only then was DreamSpace Productions formed from it as a separate venture. The loop above does not describe how the whole organisation works today.

How the academy describes its role

Two of the largest early programmes reached DreamSpace Academy through intermediaries. It was third in one delivery chain and fourth in the other, and in neither case was it the funder's grantee of record. Two rules follow for what the academy claims.

  • A sub-contracted delivery is not described as the academy's own programme. The academy says it joined as the incubator, or as the delivery partner, which names the role without claiming the mandate.
  • The academy does not write that it ran a particular funder's programme when that funder did not contract it. Anybody who checks will find an intermediary in between.

The academy has been on the receiving end of exactly the structure it now proposes to operate: incubated by one organisation, sub-contracted by two others. That is useful experience. It is not evidence that it can run the structure from the other end.

Bridging: different arrangements behind one word

The word bridging covers four arrangements with different economics, different counterparties and different failure modes. They are never added together into one number, and only one of them bears on whether the model can be replicated.

ModeWhat movesWho carries delivery riskBears on replication?
Trainer placement An individual. The funder’s own programme. The academy is not in the delivery chain. No. It is income for a person.
Delivery devolution A school cluster or a district. A local organisation, employing its own staff. Yes. This is the only mode that is shaped like a franchise.
Venue and convening Premises and standing. Whoever owns the event. No. It is hospitality, not delivery.
Exposure A network position the academy holds. Nobody. There is no delivery. No. And the people who gain from it are often the academy's own staff.

Devolution: local organisations deliver school programmes

In the second mode, the academy devolves delivery of school programmes to local organisations that run clusters with their own staff. Local capacity delivers the work, which is what was missing where one of the academy's hubs closed.

Devolution and “the model replicates” are different claims

They differ for three reasons. The academy did not build those organisations; it handed work to bodies that already existed, and devolving work is not the same as incubating it. The academy's name is on delivery it does not control, which is a quality risk. And no terms are documented for any of these clusters: not the agreement, not the money, not the quality check.

The academy says that it devolves delivery to local organisations, and those organisations can be named. It does not count their clusters as units it built.

Replication: taking a site somewhere else

The vehicle for taking a physical site somewhere else is the MakerSpace, run as a social franchise: a local partner provides the space for a community-rooted unit, and the academy provides the unit’s design, a shared brand and quality framework, and a licence to run its programmes there. The programmes themselves do not need a franchise to travel; they also run in schools, at partner sites and online.

Surplus is meant to be reinvested into the mission rather than extracted from it[48, 49, 51]. The academy designs, costs and launches nothing before it has done a site diagnosis for that particular community.

Replication sets out how that works in detail: what travels, what must be adapted locally, and what the quality framework holds people to.
