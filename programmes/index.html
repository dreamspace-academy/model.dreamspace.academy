More than the STEAM programmes

The STEAM programmes are one part of DreamSpace Academy’s education. The academy also runs business and entrepreneurship trainings, among them entrepreneurship training for women, a youth entrepreneurship campus, startup bootcamps and the DreamShot Startup Challenge. It runs the Learn to Discern media and information literacy workshops and the Digital Security School, software, web and AI bootcamps, and digital marketing training for community organisations. And it trains people already at work: workplace English for the staff of a manufacturer, leadership workshops, training of trainers, and courses for school teachers.

Every dated activity is on the activities pages. The rest of this page describes the STEAM programmes. The Changemaker Journey, from Explorer to Changemaker, is the progression used in the STEAM programmes. This site does not describe the academy’s other teaching in its terms.

The STEAM programmes: why their domains sit together

The canonical definition of maker education is itself cross-disciplinary. Dale Dougherty describes it as a physical mash-up that integrates the workshop, home economics, art and science[2], which rejects the idea that making is a branch of engineering. The education literature describes it the same way, as practices that cut across subject boundaries rather than sitting inside one[3]. European policy analysis of makerspaces in education and training reaches a similar conclusion about the range of competences involved[29].

Three things follow from teaching several domains side by side rather than as separate programmes.

1

Real problems are not single-subject

A device that helps a fisher needs electronics, a waterproof enclosure, a price and somebody who can explain it in a video. One domain solves none of that alone.

2

People arrive through different doors

Someone who will not come to a robotics session will come to record a song. Once inside, every other domain is open to them.

3

Capability transfers sideways

Iteration learned in a mixing session is the same iteration a prototype needs. What transfers between domains is the working method.

This matters most for the learners a technical programme is least likely to reach. Inclusion research finds participation improving when agency transfers to participants and non-traditional ways of knowing are valued[27]. In practice that means offering something that is not engineering.

Depth belongs to a domain, so someone can be deep in software, a beginner in electronics and midway through music. The maker profile records that rather than averaging it into one number.

Which domains are offered now

This is the current portfolio, not a fixed structure. Domains may be added, combined or retired as community interest, facilitator capability and course evidence develop. A practice area may be named here while still in development (two of those below are), but a structured course is published only once its purpose, prerequisites, learning outcomes, delivery format and assessment evidence are clear. That is why the list of domains is longer than the list of courses.

How the domains relate to each other

A domain describes what someone is learning and making. Technology is used across them wherever it improves the work, and not because a domain has been classified as technical. Music involves recording software and electronics, art involves digital fabrication, and sport involves performance analysis. The grouping below is only a way of organising the list. Software and AI also overlap with the academy’s digital skills training, and whether they belong inside the STEAM programmes has not been decided.

Maker & fabrication

The core of the STEAM programmes: turning an idea into a physical thing that works. It runs as two related arcs that are taught together.

  • Electronics and robotics: basics of electronics, microcontrollers, connected systems, embedded intelligence
  • Digital fabrication: 3D design and printing, laser cutting and engraving, CNC routing
  • Cardboard and low-cost prototyping throughout: the cheapest step, and the one that replicates without capital
Deep source curriculum

Digital education

From first contact with a computer to the digital fluency that professional and academic work assumes.

  • Basic operation: how a computer handles input, processing, output and files
  • System fundamentals, productivity software and everyday digital safety
  • Number systems, first programming, physical computing
  • Applied office work on a real project
  • Professional fluency: AI tools, proposal writing and budgeting, a public professional profile
Deep source curriculum

AI programmes

Artificial intelligence taught as something to build with and to think critically about.

  • No-code AI: no prior programming required; places and the usual participation conditions still apply
  • AI and data science fundamentals: requires programming and hardware basics
  • Generative AI and language models: requires programming maturity
  • Responsible and ethical use runs through all three, not as a closing lecture
Deep source curriculum

AI + electronics

Where the two previous domains meet: intelligence embedded in something physical. In practice this is a join rather than a ladder of its own: the upper half of the electronics arc combined with applied AI.

  • Sensors, connected systems and simple automation
  • Hardware projects that make a decision rather than follow a script
  • First innovation projects linking physical making to digital intelligence
Deep source curriculum

Software & tech-entrepreneurship

Code taught as a route to shipping something people use, including the commercial half that software courses usually leave out.

  • Static web: markup, styling, hosting something publicly
  • Front-end frameworks and interactive applications
  • Full-stack development, data and APIs
  • Ship and sell: testing, deployment, security, freelancing and running the work as a business
Deep source curriculum

Creative and physical domains

These broaden how people enter, learn and create here, and many people come in through them. Each uses digital tools, electronics, fabrication or AI wherever those serve the work.

Creative arts

Making through image, sound, performance and physical material, with traditional and digital tools treated with equal seriousness.

  • Visual art and craft, including work in upcycled and salvaged material
  • Music: playing, production, songwriting and beat-making
  • Media: photography, video, and the editing that turns footage into a story
  • Instrument-building, where craft and electronics meet
Real delivery, progression not yet published

Fitness & sports

Physical development, discipline and teamwork, in two halves.

  • A fitness studio: movement, conditioning, health and wellbeing
  • Sports and athletics: team and individual sport
  • Mind sport, anchored by chess
A design direction, limited delivery to date

What a level is

A level is a descriptor of knowledge, skill and autonomy; a volume of notional learning hours; and the assessable artifact that evidences it. A course’s internal progression is described in Units, not levels, so that “level” keeps one meaning across the whole system.

Levels belong to courses, not to people, and they exist only inside the Maker stage. Above it, progress is marked by named milestones instead. The exit condition of the Maker stage in any domain is the skilled maker: someone who can independently carry a suitable project in that domain (their own, or one that came from a community, a partner or the course) all the way to the completion standard. That is a demonstrable event, not a duration.

What deepens between one level and the next

“Harder” is not a specification. Four axes make the difference between adjacent levels checkable, and a level that has not moved along any of them is not a level. It is more of the same work with a bigger number on it.

How a level count is derived from the subject

The academy anchors the two ends: entry with no prior making, and exit as a skilled maker. Then it identifies the real capability thresholds between them, measured against the volume of one deliverable block. That takes judgement, and the result has to be justified rather than asserted. Each level has to carry its own outcomes, a defensible amount of learning, and its own assessable evidence. What this rules out is picking a number to match another subject.

Different subjects contain different numbers of thresholds, so they do not line up into equal columns, and they are not forced to.

What “Not yet” means

A published level count carries a promise: a placement check, an exit condition and an assessable artifact at every threshold. Where a subject does not yet have those, the entry reads “Not yet”, which describes the state of the documentation, not the quality of the teaching.

How labs differ from domains

Domains are what a learner progresses through. A lab is something different: a capability and a community of practice, meaning the people who share a craft, the equipment the craft needs, and a facilitator who keeps the work going. A lab is facilitated on rotation and not owned by one person, because capability that belongs to one individual leaves when they do.

A lab is not tied to a building either. It can run at the academy’s own premises, at a partner’s site, online or in a MakerSpace. No standing list of labs is published; the conditions for naming one are set out under what counts as a lab. The practice is currently grouped around four areas of work. These are areas of practice, not a list of labs.

Electromechanics

Electronics and mechanics are practised together, because most device work draws on both and separating them separates the bench from the board.

Software

Machines, a network, and quiet working time.

Media

Camera, lighting, edit stations.

Music

Instruments, recording and production.

AI is not an area of its own. It runs as a thread through all of them, because that is how it appears in real work: in the sensor board, in the edit, in the code, in the analysis. The grouping reflects the work now, and is not a permanent feature of the model.

Delivery formats

Open experiences

Explorer-stage entry experiences providing access to tools, ideas and possible domains. Unassessed, and no prior capability required. Places, safeguarding and practical conditions still apply.

Structured courses

Maker-stage courses with stated prerequisites, Units, learning outcomes and assessed work leading to a certificate.

Open making sessions

Unstructured, self-directed time with the tools. Nothing is assessed. The academy timetables these sessions rather than leaving them to spare time (see facilitation).

Challenge and project work

Present throughout the journey; at Innovator and above it carries real external context, user contact and learner responsibility, with milestones recorded rather than graded.

What every course publishes

This list is the specification. A course that cannot fill it in is not ready to be offered.

  • Purpose and prerequisites
  • Learning outcomes and expected time commitment
  • Units and capability thresholds
  • Tools, materials and safety requirements
  • Assessed artifacts, and the course’s Definition of Done gloss row
  • The placement check, and how prior learning is recognised
  • Certification evidence: what the artifact, talk-through and reflection must show
  • Version, publication date and updated date

A course document without a version and a date cannot be corrected, because nobody can tell which version a learner was taught against.

The enterprise strand runs through all of them

Inside the STEAM programmes, research and development, entrepreneurship, and business and financial literacy run across every domain rather than being taught as a separate closing module, mapped against the published entrepreneurship competence framework[36]. They are integrated where they serve the learner or the project. Enterprise capability is not the same as commercialisation, and no learner, artwork or community project is required to become a business.

The sequence is gated on prerequisites rather than age. Saving and selling are accessible very early[43]. Understanding profit depends on first grasping price comparison[45]. And the strongest randomised trial of experiential enterprise education for young people found it moved mindset rather than business knowledge[39].

So the early stages build the disposition through making and, where a learner chooses it, simple selling under the protections set out for that activity. Abstract business content attaches later, to whoever holds the prerequisite. The innovation & enterprise page sets out the full strand.

The strand is not the same thing as the academy’s business and entrepreneurship trainings. The strand is part of every course in the STEAM programmes; the trainings run on their own, as described at the top of this page.
